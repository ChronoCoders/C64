COMMODORE 1541

Not a peripheral. A second computer, with its own processor, its own memory, and its own operating system in ROM. The C64 does not read the disk. It asks the drive to, over five wires, one bit at a time.

CPU MOS 6502 @ 1.0 MHz RAM 2 KB ROM 16 KB DOS Capacity 170 KB Released 1982

TWO COMPUTERS, FIVE WIRES

The C64 and the drive each run their own program at their own clock, and neither can see the other's memory. Everything passes through the serial bus: ATN to get attention, CLK and DATA to hand over bits, one at a time, with a handshake for every single one. The two clocks are not even synchronised, which is exactly why a handshake was necessary.

INSIDE THE DRIVE

Four parts, and one of them is a full processor sitting idle most of the time, waiting to be spoken to.

6502 · PROCESSOR

MOS 6502 · the same family as the C64's, without the I/O port
1.0 MHz

Runs the DOS. It boots, tests its own memory, turns the motor off, lights the LED, and then sits in a loop waiting for the C64 to pull ATN.

  • A plain 6502: no banking port, it never needed one
  • Its clock is independent of the C64's, they drift apart
  • Fast loaders work by sending it new code to run

2 KB RAM

$0000 – $07FF
2048 bytes, that is all

Enough for the DOS variables and a couple of sector buffers. A sector is 256 bytes, so the drive can only hold a handful at once, and reads a track piece by piece.

  • Holds the sector buffers the DOS reads into
  • Small enough that Dolphin DOS added 8 KB more, so a whole track fits in one pass of the disk
  • Fast loaders upload their code into here

16 KB DOS ROM

$C000 – $FFFF · CBM DOS 2.6
a whole operating system

The drive's own OS. It knows the directory, the block map, file types, and how to turn a filename into a head position. The C64 sends it text commands, and it answers.

  • Handles LOAD, SAVE, directory, format, scratch
  • Reports status in plain English: 73, CBM DOS V2.6 1541
  • Notoriously slow, because the serial code inside it is careful, not fast

VIA 1 · THE BUS

MOS 6522 · Versatile Interface Adapter
$1800

Talks to the C64. It senses ATN, drives CLK and DATA, and interrupts the drive CPU the moment the computer wants attention.

  • The ATN interrupt: how the drive knows it is being called
  • Reads and pulls the CLK and DATA lines
  • Also carries the device number jumpers (8, 9, 10, 11)

VIA 2 · THE MECHANICS

MOS 6522 · the one wired to the hardware
$1C00

Everything physical. The motor, the stepper that moves the head, the read/write electronics, and the signal that says a byte has arrived from under the head.

  • Stepper motor: two bits, and the head walks in half-track steps
  • Motor on/off and the LED (the one that blinks red on an error)
  • BYTE READY: raw bits from the disk arrive here, in GCR
  • Write protect sense, and the density setting per zone

THE MECHANISM

read/write head, stepper, spindle
300 RPM

A 5.25 inch floppy turning at 300 revolutions a minute, and a head that slides in and out. There is no sensor telling the drive where the head is: it counts steps, and hopes.

  • No head position sensor, so on an error the DOS bangs the head against track 1 to recalibrate. That is the noise everyone remembers.
  • The banging, over time, knocked the head out of alignment
  • Copy protection deliberately used errors the drive could not reproduce

THE DISK ITSELF

35 tracks, but not the same number of sectors on each. The outer tracks are physically longer, so more data fits, and Commodore used that: the drive changes recording density as the head moves. Four zones, four densities. Click a track, or drag the head.

WHY IT WAS SO SLOW

The 1541 could move roughly 300 bytes a second out of the box. The hardware was capable of far more, and every fast loader ever written is a story about working around this.

The bus was crippled late

The design had a fast parallel scheme. It was dropped, and a slow bit-by-bit software handshake was used instead. The hardware shift register that would have made it fast sits there, unused.

a cost decision, not a technical limit

Every bit is negotiated

Sender says a bit is ready, receiver says it took it. Two signals per bit, in software, on both sides. Eight bits a byte, and the drive is also busy turning a motor.

correctness bought with time

Only 2 KB to work with

The drive cannot hold a whole track, so it reads a sector, sends it, waits, comes back for the next. The disk keeps spinning while it waits, so it misses the next sector and has to wait a full turn.

why extra RAM made drives fast

So people rewrote the drive

Since the drive is a computer, you can send it your own program. Fast loaders do exactly that: upload new code into its RAM, then talk to it in a protocol Commodore never wrote.

the drive's openness was its rescue
